### Two Industries, One Shrinking Talent Pool

A car built in 2018 carried a handful of control units. Today’s models carry dozens, along with driver assistance, battery management, connected dashboards, and over-the-air updates. Each of these needs engineers who understand hardware and code together.

Meanwhile, GCCs in Gurgaon, set up by international automotive and technology firms, are moving real product work to India. Design, testing, and software ownership now sit here, not just support tasks.

The result is a tug of war. A tier-one supplier in Manesar and a GCC in Cyber Hub may want the same embedded software engineer with five years behind them. That engineer gets six calls a week and has learnt to ignore most. Recruiters who forward keyword-matched profiles rarely get a reply. Recruiters who speak the engineer’s language do.

### The Problems Hiring Managers Keep Describing

Ask any plant head or engineering director this year and the same complaints surface:

- **Talent shortage:** specialists in battery systems, vehicle networks, and functional safety are scarce, and most are already employed.
- **Unverified candidates:** a polished resume may claim ownership of a project the person only touched for two weeks.
- **High employee turnover:** a hire who joined for a salary bump, rather than for the work, tends to leave when the next offer arrives.

The lengthy hiring process adds its own damage. Three rounds stretched over six weeks hand good candidates to competitors. And every empty seat raises operational costs: a delayed validation cycle pushes a vehicle programme back, and a rushed replacement hire costs more than the careful one would have.

### Screening That Goes Beyond the Resume

A consultancy that understands this space does not start with a database search. It starts with a conversation about what the role truly demands on day one, which tools the engineer will use, and which problems are waiting on the desk.

From there, the sequence is deliberate. A resume review checks career logic, not only keywords: does the progression make sense, and do the projects match the claimed seniority? A telephonic or video screening follows, where a recruiter with an engineering ear asks about real work, such as a fault the candidate traced or a design trade-off they defended.

Then comes an assignment test shaped to the opening. A hardware role may get a schematic review; a software role may get a short debugging task. Nothing here is meant to trick anyone. It simply shows what the resume cannot.

Clients then receive detailed candidate insights: strengths, gaps, communication style, and the candidate’s own reasons for moving. Salary negotiation is the last step, handled openly so that no one is surprised at the offer stage. ### Matching the Engineer to the Team, Not Just the Opening

Auto-electronics suppliers and GCCs hire differently, and a good recruiter respects that difference.

A supplier plant usually values hands-on speed, comfort with production-line realities, and a steady temperament under customer deadlines. A GCC usually values structured thinking, documentation habits and the ability to work with colleagues in other time zones. An engineer who thrives in one setting can struggle in the other.

So the matching conversation includes the unglamorous questions. How large is the team? Who will the new joiner report to? What does a normal week look like? Is the product roadmap stable? Candidates who hear honest answers before joining are far less likely to feel misled three months later, which protects the client’s investment.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. Which roles does an auto-electronics hiring partner usually fill?**

Embedded software, hardware design, validation, power electronics, battery management, and functional safety roles are the usual ones. Supplier plants also need quality and production engineers, while GCCs lean toward software, simulation, and systems positions. The screening should always be adapted to each role’s actual demands.

**Q2. Can a consultancy hire for GCC teams and supplier plants alike?**

Yes, provided it treats them as two different environments. GCC hiring emphasises structured problem-solving and collaboration across time zones, while plant hiring emphasises practical speed and production awareness. A capable consultancy changes its questions, assignments and candidate conversations for each, instead of reusing one template everywhere.

**Q3. How long does a technical shortlist usually take?**

With a pre-verified talent pool and a clear brief, a first shortlist can often reach the client within a few working days. Niche profiles, such as functional safety specialists, may take longer. Honest timelines at the start prevent disappointment and let managers plan interviews properly.

**Q4. How are unverified candidates filtered out?**

Through layered checks: resume review, a live screening conversation, a role-specific assignment, and a closer look at project ownership. Claims that cannot be explained in detail rarely survive the second step, so clients end up meeting fewer candidates, but noticeably stronger ones.

**Q5. What reduces early resignations after joining?**

Honest role briefings, realistic salary discussions, and regular check-ins after the joining date. When candidates understand the team, the work, and the pay before accepting, surprises shrink. Early follow-up catches small frictions while they are still easy to fix, which keeps good engineers longer.
